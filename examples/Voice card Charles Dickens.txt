VOICE CARD: CHARLES DICKENS
Narrative stance. Write as an omniscient third-person narrator who is warm but not neutral — he has opinions about his characters and lets them leak through, via a raised eyebrow of an adjective, a mock-solemn aside, or a sudden shift into direct address to the reader ("Let us confess it," "the reader will observe"). This narrator sees into people's private vanities and self-deceptions before they see it themselves, and enjoys pointing it out gently rather than cruelly — except toward genuine hypocrisy or cruelty, where the irony sharpens considerably. The narrator is never a neutral camera; he editorializes, but through implication and tone more than blunt statement.
Sentence architecture. Favor long, cumulative sentences built from clause piled on clause, joined by semicolons, dashes, and coordinating conjunctions rather than broken into short modern sentences. A single sentence can comfortably run eight or ten lines, especially in descriptive or comic passages, gathering details in threes and building to a small payoff at the end. Balance this against short, blunt sentences used deliberately for emphasis, especially at moments of shock, menace, or emotional turn — the contrast is part of the effect. Use semicolons and dashes generously; use parenthetical asides to slip in a wry observation mid-sentence without breaking its flow.
Characterization technique. Introduce a character through a small cluster of vivid, physical, slightly exaggerated details — a gesture, a habit, an item of clothing, a verbal tic — and then return to that same detail each time the character reappears, almost like a musical motif (Jasper's watchfulness and his "silent" way of listening; Grewgious's dry precision and his habit of speaking as though reading a legal clause; Sapsea's pomposity). Physical description should imply moral or psychological character rather than simply itemizing appearance — a face, a walk, a way of shaking hands should tell the reader something about the soul inside. Minor and comic characters may tip into caricature and repeated catchphrases; major characters (Jasper, Rosa, Neville, Helena) should be drawn with more interiority and ambiguity, their true feelings visible to the reader in flashes even when hidden from other characters.
Dialogue. Give each character a distinct idiolect — Durdles's rough workingman's speech and grumbling asides, Sapsea's self-important legal-sounding pronouncements, Miss Twinkleton's genteel euphemism, Deputy's street patter, Grewgious's fussy precision, Crisparkle's plain cheerful directness. Dialogue should carry social class and personality as much as plot information. Avoid modern contractions and idiom; favor period-appropriate courtesy forms of address and the small formalities of 1870s English speech, without tipping into parody.
Humor and irony. Dickens's comedy comes from incongruity, self-importance being punctured, and precise absurd detail — a pompous man undone by his own words, a minor character whose priorities are hilariously misplaced given the situation. Even in the darker chapters of Drood, allow small pockets of comic relief (Sapsea, Mrs. Tope, Deputy) to relieve tension before it resumes; Dickens rarely sustains unbroken gravity for long.
Description and atmosphere. Setting is never just backdrop. Use pathetic fallacy freely — weather, light, and the physical fabric of Cloisterham (fog, the cathedral's shadows, the crypt's chill, a storm on Christmas Eve) should mirror or foreshadow the emotional undercurrent of a scene. Personify inanimate things lightly and playfully elsewhere (a wind that "goes about like a beggar," a house that "seems to know its own secrets") but let the personification turn genuinely ominous in Drood's gothic passages — the cathedral, the crypt, and the opium den should feel almost sentient, watching.
What to avoid. No modern vocabulary, slang, or contractions of the "don't/can't" type used more sparingly than in contemporary prose (Dickens uses some, but less freely, and never modern slang). No anachronistic technology or social attitudes. Avoid flatly explaining a character's psychology in plain modern terms ("he felt anxious because...") — show it through behavior, gesture, and the narrator's arch commentary instead. Avoid short, staccato modern paragraphing throughout; Dickens's paragraphs are often substantial. Above all, resist the urge to resolve ambiguity too quickly — the essence of continuing Drood well is maintaining Dickens's own careful, teasing withholding of the truth.
